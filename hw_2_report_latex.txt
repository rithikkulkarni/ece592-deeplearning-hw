\section{Question 1}

I implemented \texttt{train\_tfm} by stacking four augmentation transforms on top of the required resize/tensor conversion, resizing to $128\times128$ (matching the sample code's default, so no changes were needed to the classifier's flattened input dimension):

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Final train\_tfm}, label={lst:train-tfm}]
train_tfm = transforms.Compose([
    transforms.RandomResizedCrop(128, scale=(0.7, 1.0)),
    transforms.RandomHorizontalFlip(),
    transforms.RandomRotation(15),
    transforms.ColorJitter(brightness=0.2, contrast=0.2, saturation=0.2),
    transforms.ToTensor(),
])
\end{lstlisting}

To confirm this concretely satisfies the "5+ different results" requirement rather than just assuming the transforms are random enough, I applied \texttt{train\_tfm} to the same training image 5 times and plotted the results alongside the original (\autoref{fig:aug-grid}). I additionally computed the pairwise mean absolute difference between all 5 output tensors, which ranged from 0.07 to 0.20 -- confirming every pair is meaningfully distinct rather than near-duplicate.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{q1_augmentation_grid.png}
    \caption{Original training image (leftmost) and 5 independent applications of train\_tfm to it.}
    \label{fig:aug-grid}
\end{figure}

Each transform in the stack targets a distinct source of overfitting:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RandomResizedCrop} randomly crops a sub-region (70--100\% of the image area) before resizing to $128\times128$. This teaches the model that a food class shouldn't depend on the object being perfectly centered or filling the whole frame, countering overfitting to the exact framing/composition of the training photos.
    \item \textbf{RandomHorizontalFlip} mirrors the image left-right with 50\% probability. Food photos have no canonical "handedness," so this teaches left-right invariance for free and effectively doubles the visual variety of each image across training epochs.
    \item \textbf{RandomRotation(15)} rotates the image by up to $\pm15^\circ$. This counters overfitting to the exact camera angle/tilt a photo happened to be taken at. A small angle range was chosen deliberately to avoid distorting food identity, unlike larger rotations (e.g. $90^\circ$) which could make some dishes look unnatural or ambiguous.
    \item \textbf{ColorJitter} randomly perturbs brightness, contrast, and saturation. This counters overfitting to the specific lighting conditions and white balance of individual photos, which matters significantly for a food dataset where lighting and photography source vary a lot across images.
\end{itemize}

Note that \texttt{transforms.Compose} applies all four transforms every call, in the same fixed order -- nothing is randomly skipped at the transform level (with the partial exception of \texttt{RandomHorizontalFlip}'s internal coin flip). The visible diversity in \autoref{fig:aug-grid} instead comes from each transform independently drawing new random parameters (crop region, rotation angle, jitter factors) on every call.
